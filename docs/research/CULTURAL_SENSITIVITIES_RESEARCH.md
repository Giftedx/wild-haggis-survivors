# Cultural Sensitivities & Ethics — Wild Haggis Survivors

> *"Scotland is a real place with real complexity. The masterpiece bar of the Soul Charter demands we handle it with love and accuracy."*
> — From the first Scottish research doc

> **Purpose.** A practical ethics and sensitivity reference for a Scottish-themed game aspiring to masterpiece quality. Scotland is not a costume — it is a living culture with open historical wounds, marginalised language communities, ongoing political debates, and real trademarks. This doc identifies where WHS has to tread carefully, *why*, and *how*.
>
> **How this relates to other docs.**
> - `SCOTTISH_RESEARCH.md` and `SCOTTISH_RESEARCH_DEEP.md` — content ideas.
> - `DESIGN_SOUL.md` — north-star principles (warmth, kindness, handcraft).
> - `VOICE_CARD.md` — voice and anti-patterns.
> - **This doc** — *the ethical filter* for any Scottish content before it ships.
>
> **How to use.** Before writing any spec, banter line, biome, boss, variant, or event that touches a sensitive area (listed in Part 2), consult the relevant section. If you find yourself thinking "is this respectful?" — *you're probably in a Part 2 or Part 3 topic* and the answer is usually "consult more before shipping."
>
> **Tone.** This doc is *not* a dossier of things not to do. It's a celebration of Scotland *done properly* — which requires knowing the terrain. Most of these sensitivities have *good* ways to engage; this doc surfaces both the pitfalls and the respectful paths.

## Foundational Principles

Four rules underpin everything that follows. They are non-negotiable and derive directly from the Soul Charter:

1. **Affection before accuracy, accuracy before cleverness.** We write from love of the place. When love and cleverness conflict, love wins.
2. **Scottish-made comedy about Scotland is the model.** Still Game, Limmy, Chewin' the Fat. Scots self-mocking from within is warm. Outsider-mocking from without is not. When in doubt, stay inside the tradition.
3. **Consult the community when the stakes are live.** Gaelic speakers, Traveller community members, Northern Isles representatives, living descendants of cleared families. Small fees for consultation are always worth it; ship nothing culturally-specific without one pair of native-informed eyes.
4. **Handcraft is not optional.** The Soul Charter demands handcrafted care. AI-generated cultural content without human curation fails this test. Every Scottish reference must be written, checked, and loved by a human.

## Part 1 — The "Outsider Writing Scotland" Problem

### 1.1 The pitfall

Scotland is *heavily* tourist-consumed. The Disney-fied Scotland — shortbread-tin postcard kilts, "och aye the noo", Nessie-hat tourism — is a real phenomenon that Scots are generally good-natured about but *deeply* aware of. A Scottish-themed game from an outsider perspective can *easily* fall into this trap:

- Tartan as costume, not craft.
- Bagpipes as joke, not instrument.
- Gaelic as mystical runes, not living language.
- Highlanders as noble savages, not people.
- The Highland Clearances as "colourful backstory", not a trauma still being processed.
- Burns as a quotable rather than a socialist poet.
- Glasgow as comic violence, not post-industrial love.

### 1.2 The test

Ask of any Scottish reference in the game: **"Would a Scot watching over your shoulder wince or grin?"**

- **Grin** = on-voice, loved, correct. Keep.
- **Wince** = reconsider. Doesn't mean remove — means *go deeper* or *rework*.
- **Pain / offence** = don't ship.

### 1.3 What "from the inside" looks like

- **Buckfast jokes are fine** — Scots make them constantly.
- **Haggis hunter jokes are fine** — the wild haggis myth *is* Scots prank culture.
- **Dreich weather jokes are fine** — universal Scots bond.
- **Class commentary (Glasgow working-class, Edinburgh middle-class posh)** is fine when Still-Game-warm, not sneering.
- **Dialect comedy** (Doric "fit like?" confusion, Glasgow patter) is fine when *celebrated*, not mocked.
- **Food comedy** (deep-fried Mars bar, square sausage rolls) is fine — Scots self-mock these endlessly.

### 1.4 What "from outside" looks like (avoid)

- "Och aye the noo" — no Scot says this; only adverts.
- "Bonnie wee lassie" stacked cliché.
- Every character kilted always.
- Treating Gaelic as spooky mystic language.
- Reducing the Cailleach to a generic witch.
- "Braveheart face-paint" imagery without historical grounding.
- Treating the 1745 Jacobite defeat as a jolly historical setting.
- Playing the Highland Clearances as "adventure backdrop."

### 1.5 Practical guidance

**Before writing any Scottish-flavoured content**, ask:
1. Is this something a Scot would say about themselves?
2. Is this something a Scot would say about another Scot warmly?
3. Is this something a Scot would hear with a smile?

If yes to all three → proceed. If no to any → consult `VOICE_CARD.md` anti-patterns and this doc.

## Part 2 — Handling Historical Trauma

Scotland's history contains real trauma. Events where people died, communities were destroyed, languages were suppressed. These cannot be light-touch backdrop.

### 2.1 The Highland Clearances (c. 1760–1860)

**What happened.** Landlords (often clan chiefs) systematically cleared their tenant farmers from ancestral lands, replacing them with sheep for higher rents. Hundreds of thousands emigrated to North America, Australia, New Zealand. Homes were burned; communities destroyed; families divided. Sutherland Clearances (1811–21) under the Countess of Sutherland's factor Patrick Sellar included houses burned with tenants inside. Gaelic language and culture decimated.

**Why it's still sensitive.** The Clearances are *living memory* in many Highland communities. Descendants track surnames, lost villages, lost bloodlines. The Duke of Sutherland statue at Ben Bhraggie has been repeatedly vandalised and the subject of campaigns to topple. Land reform remains politically active in Scotland.

**Handling guidance:**
- **Do not** use Clearance villages as "atmospheric ruins" for haggis-run decor without narrative weight.
- **Do not** romanticise "Highland bloodlines" in ways that sound ethno-nationalist.
- **Do** acknowledge cleared communities as *real* — if a ruined-croft biome exists, the ruins should be *seen as loss*, not as set-dressing.
- **Do** lean on memorialisation references: Gerald Laing's *Exiles* sculpture (Helmsdale, 2007), the Strathnaver memorials, the Donald MacLeod "Gloomy Memories" tradition.
- **Do** cite, if using a Clearance-themed biome, the specific location and that this was a real event. Small in-game placard works.
- **Tone register:** always *Grave* (per DESIGN_SOUL tonal spectrum). Never Wild Comedy. Never Hearth.

### 2.2 Glencoe Massacre (1692)

**What happened.** On 13 February 1692, Campbell of Glenlyon's soldiers — government troops who had been sheltered and fed in MacDonald homes for 12 days — slaughtered 38 MacDonalds (some sources: 30) of Glencoe in the early hours, including women and children. Reason: MacIain, the MacDonald chief, had been late in swearing loyalty to William III. More MacDonalds died in the mountains that winter fleeing. A specific, politically-motivated massacre under colour of law.

**Why it's still sensitive.** "Murder Under Trust" violated basic Gaelic hospitality codes; the Gaelic phrase *mì-rùn mòr nan Gall* ("great ill-will of the Lowlander") encompasses this. Campbells have been "traitor clan" in folk memory for three centuries (reality is more complex; Campbells as a group were not monolithic). Glen Coe today is a *sombre* place; visitors are expected to feel weight.

**Handling guidance:**
- **Do not** make "Glencoe" a comedic or action-backdrop name.
- **Do not** cast Campbells as stock villains without context.
- **Do not** use the massacre as a "hook" for gameplay novelty.
- **Do** treat a Glencoe-themed biome with Grave tonality.
- **Do** consider the *natural sublime* of Glen Coe (it's also famously beautiful) alongside historical weight.
- **Avoid** a specific event-day playable: "run the massacre" framing is offensive.
- **Consider** instead: a memorial moment — Gran whispers of the MacDonalds, a photograph of the valley, a route name like *"Ghost of the Glen"* with sombre banter acknowledging what happened without re-enacting it.

### 2.3 Culloden and the '45 Aftermath (1746 onwards)

**What happened.** The Battle of Culloden (16 April 1746) on Drumossie Moor near Inverness ended the Jacobite Rising of 1745. Government troops under William, Duke of Cumberland, defeated Charles Edward Stuart's Highland army in less than an hour. Cumberland then ordered systematic repression — indiscriminate killings of Highlanders (combatants and non-combatants), destruction of Highland economy, banning of tartan and clan names, enforced Anglicisation. "The Butcher" became Cumberland's popular name. Culloden is bleak; the visitor centre emphasises this.

**Why it's still sensitive.** Cumberland's post-battle policy was *state policy targeting Gaelic culture*. This is the start of the Highland-as-exotic-backdrop framing (Scott, Romantic era). Many Highland-diaspora families trace their emigration lineage to post-Culloden.

**Handling guidance:**
- **Do not** make Bonnie Prince Charlie an uncomplicated dashing hero. He was politically naïve, ran away after defeat, and his rising cost thousands of Highland lives.
- **Do not** frame Culloden as "the last great battle on British soil" for cosplay value.
- **Do** treat the '45 as tragic, period. Heroism existed on both sides and in non-combatants.
- **Do** be careful with "redcoat" cosplay — they were British soldiers following orders; they were also responsible for atrocities.
- **Consider** Flora MacDonald (see `SCOTTISH_RESEARCH_DEEP.md` §6.9) as a more complex heroic figure than Charlie.
- **A Jacobite-themed biome** should be sombre, and its banter should acknowledge *human cost* on both sides — not just triumphal nostalgia.

### 2.4 The Killing Times (1679–88)

**What happened.** Government persecution of Covenanters (Scottish Presbyterians who refused Episcopal reforms). Bloody Clavers (John Graham of Claverhouse) hunted Covenanter preachers. Hundreds executed or transported. Martyrs like Margaret Wilson (drowned at age 18 in Solway Firth) are still commemorated in southwest Scotland.

**Why it's still sensitive.** Presbyterian Scotland's founding-trauma in living religious memory. The covenanter martyrs are real-historical-saints for many Scottish Protestants.

**Handling guidance:**
- **Rare/respectful** is the mode.
- If used: sombre Grave tone, respectful memorial framing.
- No "Covenanter enemies" as cosmetic evil.

### 2.5 Flodden (1513)

**What happened.** Scotland's worst military defeat. King James IV and most of Scotland's nobility (many senior clerics too) died on the field in a battle with England. The "Flowers of the Forest" song commemorates it.

**Why it's significant.** *The loss of a generation* — still referenced in Scottish cultural memory. Affects Border and Lothian identity particularly.

**Handling guidance:** Can be referenced, but Grave tone. "Flowers of the Forest" is a permission-free public-domain pibroch lament.

### 2.6 Wars of Independence (1296–1356)

**What happened.** William Wallace, Robert the Bruce, Bannockburn — foundational mythology. Also: real people executed, cities sacked (Berwick 1296 was particularly horrific), long cycles of raid-and-counter-raid.

**Why it's OK to engage more freely.** Far enough in the past to be national-founding-mythology rather than living trauma. Bannockburn victory day (23–24 June) is genuinely celebrated.

**Handling guidance:**
- Wallace and Bruce as heroes is fine.
- **But** Braveheart got a lot wrong — kilts weren't worn yet, Wallace wasn't a farmer, etc. Cite history, not Mel Gibson.
- Declaration of Arbroath (1320) is a beautiful primary source; its "for as long as but a hundred of us remain alive" line is quotable freely.

### 2.7 Specific sites to avoid or approach carefully

- **Lockerbie** (1988 terrorist bombing) — do not reference; living trauma.
- **Dunblane** (1996 school shooting) — do not reference; living trauma.
- **Aberfan** (1966 — not Scotland, Wales, but adjacent British cultural memory).
- **Piper Alpha** (1988 North Sea oil disaster) — sensitive in Aberdeen.
- **Ibrox Disaster** (1971 football stand collapse) — sensitive in Glasgow Rangers community.

### 2.8 Handling rule of thumb

Historical trauma that happened **<100 years ago** is usually living family memory. Handle with extreme care or avoid.

Trauma **100–300 years** is cultural memory — can be engaged with, respectfully.

Trauma **>300 years** can be mythologised (Wallace/Bruce) but still needs accuracy.

**No historical atrocity** should ever be used as game flavour without weight.

## Part 3 — Community Consultation Protocols

When content touches a specific community, native consultation isn't nice-to-have — it's table stakes.

### 3.1 The Gaelic-speaking community

**Situation.** ~69,701 Gaelic speakers in Scotland (2022 census). Major strongholds: Outer Hebrides (especially Uists, Lewis, Harris, Barra), parts of Skye, Tiree, smaller mainland pockets. Active revitalisation movement (Sabhal Mòr Ostaig, Gaelic-medium schools, BBC Alba, Mòd festival).

**Why consultation matters.** Gaelic has been actively suppressed for centuries (Education Act 1872, Dress Act 1746–82). Outsider use of Gaelic without speaker consultation risks being seen as extractive — using a community's suppressed language as decoration while that community fights to keep it alive.

**Consultation path.**
- **Bòrd na Gàidhlig** — the Gaelic language body. Formal consultation possible.
- **Sabhal Mòr Ostaig** (Skye) — Gaelic college; has a media/arts department.
- **Gaelic-medium primary schools** — teachers are often consultants.
- **BBC Alba** — programme-makers are excellent translators.
- **Scottish Gaelic Dictionaries / DASG** — academic.
- **Individual speakers** — often most authentic, especially Hebridean natives.

**Fees.** Gaelic translators typically charge per word. Budget for consultation from the start. Never ship Gaelic text written by an LLM without native-speaker review.

**Minimum bar for WHS:**
- Any Gaelic phrase in shipped content reviewed by at least one native speaker.
- Cailleach variant banter (already includes Gaelic phrases) should have had specific review.
- Any new Gaelic banter pool (Hebridean variant is the leading candidate) gets its own consultation pass.
- Place names in Scottish Gaelic used correctly (always spell consistently; *An t-Eilean Sgitheanach* not *Skye* in Gaelic-speaking biomes).

### 3.2 The Scottish Traveller community

**Situation.** Scottish Travellers (distinct from Irish Travellers, Romany Gypsies, New Travellers) — indigenous Scottish itinerant community with distinct language (Scottish Cant / Beurla Reagaird), storytelling traditions (Belle Stewart, Jeannie Robertson, Sheila Stewart), music (bothy ballads via Traveller singers).

**Why consultation matters.** Travellers are *extremely* media-misrepresented. UK media coverage is "disproportionately negative" (Equality and Human Rights Commission), with 48% of coverage classified as overly negative portrayals. This has "significant knock-on effects on the community, especially children in schools." Traveller children face bullying and discrimination partly because of media stereotypes.

**Common traps:**
- Travellers as criminals/thieves in fiction.
- Traveller stereotypes confused with Romani Gypsy stereotypes.
- "My Big Fat Gypsy Wedding"-style exoticism.
- Using "gypsy" as a synonym for "wanderer" in game text.

**WHS opportunity.** Scottish Travellers *preserve* most of the ballad tradition and much oral folklore — the School of Scottish Studies archive is largely Traveller-recorded. If WHS features oral tradition or ballad-style banter, acknowledge this heritage. Consider:
- A Traveller-coded NPC as a storyteller/bard (respectfully drawn, not stereotyped).
- Ballad fragments credited to Traveller tradition in an optional Almanac / Codex entry.

**Consultation path:**
- **Article 12 Scotland** — Scottish Traveller advocacy.
- **Elphinstone Institute** (University of Aberdeen) — Scottish folklore scholarship, heavy Traveller material.
- **Scottish Traveller Education Programme.**

### 3.3 The Northern Isles (Orkney & Shetland)

**Situation.** Distinct identity from "mainland Scotland." Orkney and Shetland were Norwegian until 1468; Norse-inflected culture, dialects (Shetlandic, Orcadian with Norn influence). Shetlanders in particular often identify "not as Scottish, but as Shetlanders."

**Why consultation matters.** These communities are protective of their distinctive heritage. Shetlandic's 2025 recognition as a separate ISO language code reflects serious community work. Conflating Shetland with "Scotland generally" causes friction.

**Handling:**
- If WHS adds Shetland content (Up Helly Aa biome, Peerie Haggis variant), consult local.
- Shetland Museum and Archives.
- Shetland ForWirds (dialect body).
- *The Shetland Times* — local paper with cultural writers.

### 3.4 The Lowlands / Borders / Northeast distinctions

**Situation.** WHS currently heavily leans Highlands. "Scotland" in the game means "Highland fantasy + urban Glasgow." This misses the Borders (rugby, textile, ballad tradition), Aberdeenshire (Doric, fishing, granite), Fife, Lothians, Dumfries & Galloway.

**Why it matters.** Scotland is not monolithic. A Scot from Hawick has a different identity from one in Stornoway.

**Consultation opportunity:** even *picking up a regional food* (e.g., Forfar Bridie) respectfully is a recognition move. Currently, the game could lean more into distinct regional voices.

### 3.5 Disability representation

Scottish culture — like British broadly — has its own disability-rights history. Flora MacDonald lived with Parkinson's in old age; Walter Scott had polio. Contemporary Scotland has active disability-rights groups.

**WHS opportunity:** a variant or NPC with an acknowledged condition *treated with dignity* (not as quirky game mechanic) would be both respectful and narratively rich. Burns's brother Gilbert lived with an injury that prevented him from farming. Historical precedents exist.

**Avoid:**
- Disability as "punishment" mechanic.
- Disabled-coded enemies (hump-backed, limping, drooling).
- Cailleach's hagishness as "ugliness" — she's *elder*, which is different.

### 3.6 LGBTQ+ in Scottish culture

Scotland has been relatively progressive on LGBTQ+ issues — same-sex marriage legal since 2014, strong rights framework. Some Scottish public figures (Nicola Sturgeon's leadership era, Hamza Yousaf's government) were firm supporters. Also:

- Muriel Spark's complex personal life.
- Jackie Kay (Makar, biracial, lesbian).
- Iain Banks's (discreet) LGBTQ+ themes.

**WHS opportunity:**
- Non-binary / queer-coded NPCs are welcome.
- Haggis is a fictional creature — gender-neutral by default.
- Avoid heteronormative default in NPC relationships.
- Scottish LGBTQ+ history could be referenced (Section 2A / Section 28 was overturned in Scotland in 2000, first in UK).

## Part 4 — Language & Dialect Representation

### 4.1 Scots as a language (not a dialect)

**The political point.** Scots is classified by linguists as a *language* (sister to English). It has its own literature (Burns, MacDiarmid, Lewis Grassic Gibbon), grammar rules, vocabulary. Scots is recognised under the European Charter for Regional/Minority Languages.

**Common mistake:** treating Scots as "badly-spelled English" or "Glasgow accent" or "dialect." This diminishes it.

**WHS practice:**
- Treat Scots text seriously. Bilingual rendering (English / Scots) as in existing i18n system respects Scots as a real language.
- The EN ↔ SCS parity fence in CI is a *political* act of respect as well as a technical one. Keep it.
- When a Scots line doesn't have an English equivalent, that's fine — the language has expressions English lacks (*dreich*, *coorie*, *tiptous*, etc.).

### 4.2 Gaelic usage — high care required

Per Part 3.1. Specifically:

- **Never** use Gaelic as spooky/alien decoration.
- **Never** machine-translate and ship.
- **Never** mix Gaelic and Irish Gaelic by accident. They are related but distinct languages.
- **Always** translate in-context or subtitle. Untranslated Gaelic for mystical-mood effect is appropriation.
- **Cailleach's Gaelic phrases** (currently in game): *dè do bheachd?* (what do you think?), *cò às a tha thu?* (where are you from?) — these are natural conversational phrases, well-chosen. Continue this pattern.
- **Place names** used in Gaelic form when in Hebridean/Gaelic biomes: *An t-Eilean Sgitheanach* (Skye), *Eilean Bharraigh* (Barra), *Eilean Leòdhais agus na Hearadh* (Lewis and Harris). Bilingual labels welcome.

### 4.3 Regional dialect representation

Scottish regional dialects (Doric, Shetlandic, Orcadian, Glaswegian, Hebridean English, Borders, Edinburgh) carry identity. Depicting them requires care.

**Do:**
- Research specific dialect vocabulary (SCOTTISH_RESEARCH_DEEP.md §14 has a regional glossary).
- Use dialect *authentically* — a single correct Doric phrase ("fit like?") beats ten invented ones.
- Celebrate dialect variety — ten variant haggis speaking different dialects is a love letter to Scotland.

**Don't:**
- Treat one dialect as "comic other" while another is "normal." All Scottish dialects are equally Scottish.
- Mock comprehension difficulty. Doric being incomprehensible to Glaswegians is a Scottish joke *made by Scots*; if a non-Scot writes it, consult.
- Default Glasgow as "Scottish" — it's *one* Scottish voice among many.

### 4.4 Accent comedy — pitfalls

**Acceptable:** Scots cracking jokes about other Scots' accents (Aberdonian vs Glaswegian mutual incomprehension; Doric vs Standard). Classic comedy material; Still Game does it, Limmy does it, every Scottish family does it.

**Not acceptable:** outsider mocking Scottish accent comprehension (Americans mocking Scottish incomprehensibility). Avoid. The Scottish-person-voice-ordering-a-Big-Mac trope is worn out.

**WHS application:** Burnistoun's "ELEVEN!" voice-recognition-in-lift sketch is the benchmark for *internal* accent comedy. It's a Scottish show about the world not understanding a Scottish accent, and *the joke is on the technology*, not the Scot. This kind of reversal is gold.

## Part 5 — Trademark & IP Considerations

Real-world references to branded products bring legal risk. WHS already uses several.

### 5.1 Buckfast Tonic Wine ("Buckie")

**Situation.** Made by Buckfast Abbey (a charity holding the trademark; licensed to Chandlers Wine Merchants for marketing). Notorious in Scotland for association with youth culture, Glasgow/Ayrshire working-class drinking, and occasional social controversy. Despite criticism, the Abbey has so far declined to reformulate.

**WHS usage:** `buckfast_ned` enemy (shipped), `buckie_pitstop` route (shipped), Buckfast reference in banter.

**Risk assessment.**
- **Trademark:** using the *name* "Buckfast" in a critical/satirical context falls generally under nominative fair use in UK / EU jurisdictions. Not actionable as long as: we don't imply endorsement, we don't use the actual trademark logo, we don't suggest health claims.
- **Defamation / trade disparagement:** low risk. The Buckie Ned depiction is *culturally resonant* — associating Buckfast with a specific urban archetype is a widely-shared Scottish association, not a WHS invention.
- **Recommended practice:**
  - Keep "Buckfast" references genericised where possible ("fortified wine", "bottle of bucky").
  - Avoid the actual red-and-gold Buckfast logo.
  - Keep tone comedic/observational, not defamatory.
  - Consider consulting a Scottish solicitor for a review letter if going to wide commercial release.

### 5.2 Irn-Bru

**Situation.** A.G. Barr plc, Cumbernauld. Strong brand, active trademark. Iconic orange colour; Scotland's "other national drink."

**WHS usage:** `irn_bru` passive item (shipped), Irn-Bru as cultural reference.

**Risk assessment.**
- **Trademark:** usage *not* OK in commercial context without licence. A.G. Barr protects the name aggressively.
- **Recommended practice:**
  - **Genericise:** "orange ginger soda", "Scottish soft drink", "fizzy pop".
  - **Alternative:** license the name (not trivial; small games rarely do this).
  - **Workaround:** invent a fictional near-analogue ("Iron Bull" or similar with a nod-and-wink to the real thing).
  - **Decision point:** discuss with legal counsel before wide launch.

### 5.3 Tunnock's products (Tea Cake, Caramel Wafer, Snowball)

**Situation.** Uddingston family firm. Strong brand. Generally fan-friendly.

**WHS usage potential:** food-pickup items proposed in DESIGN_IDEAS.

**Risk assessment.**
- **Trademark:** usage *not* OK without licence.
- **Recommended practice:**
  - Genericise: "caramel wafer", "chocolate-marshmallow", "Scottish tea biscuit".
  - Alternative: actually approach Tunnock's for a licensing agreement (small, family-owned; may be approachable).
- **Note:** a licensing agreement could be a *positive* marketing moment ("Wild Haggis Survivors × Tunnock's!").

### 5.4 Other brands to watch

- **Walker's Shortbread** — family firm; strong brand.
- **Edinburgh Gin, Hendrick's, individual whisky brands** — all tightly protected. Genericise to "gin", "whisky".
- **Runrig, Simple Minds, Proclaimers** — music rights; don't use song titles as feature names without licence.
- **Still Game, Limmy's Show, Trainspotting** — likeness and script rights; references should be generic inspiration, not direct quotation.
- **Local football clubs** (Celtic, Rangers, Aberdeen, Hearts, Hibs, etc.) — trademark-protected and sensitive; avoid direct usage.

### 5.5 Public domain & permission-free

Good news — much Scottish material is completely free to use:

**Public domain (Burns, Scott, Stevenson etc. per UK copyright rules — life + 70 years):**
- All Robert Burns works (died 1796).
- Walter Scott novels (died 1832).
- Robert Louis Stevenson novels (died 1894).
- Traditional folk ballads, pibroch compositions.
- The Declaration of Arbroath.
- Folk-tales (though specific collectors' versions may be in copyright).

**Traditional tartans:**
- Clan tartans are generally traditional designs in the public domain.
- **Exception:** some *specific weaver patterns* are copyright (e.g., the Harris Tweed design is trademark-protected in some forms). A generic Stewart tartan is fine; a *specific* fashion-designer tartan may not be.

**Pictish symbols, Celtic knotwork:**
- Traditional symbol-vocabulary is public domain.
- Specific modern-artist knotwork designs may be copyright.

### 5.6 Likeness of living persons

- **Janey Godley** (d. 2024) — can reference respectfully; estate may have opinions.
- **Limmy** (living) — references should be inspiration (the Edge voice register in the game draws on him), not impersonation.
- **Nicola Sturgeon, Alex Salmond** — avoid political satire as shipped content; volatile.
- **Andy Murray, Chris Hoy, etc.** — likeness rights protected.

**Rule:** *inspiration without impersonation*. Our Edge voice is Limmy-inspired, not Limmy. Our Gran is Still-Game-warm, not Jack or Victor themselves.

## Part 6 — Religious & Political Sensitivities

### 6.1 Sectarianism (Celtic / Rangers)

**Situation.** Glasgow's Celtic FC (Catholic-Irish heritage) and Rangers FC (Protestant-Unionist heritage) rivalry goes deep — sectarian chanting, violence, discrimination in hiring, etc. are real historical-and-still-active concerns. The Scottish Government has legislated against it (Offensive Behaviour at Football Act 2012, repealed 2018 but concern remains).

**WHS practice:**
- **Do not** reference Celtic vs Rangers.
- **Do not** use sectarian-coded colours in factional conflict.
- **Do not** use religious slurs (Fenian, Hun, etc.) in banter.
- **Be careful** with religious iconography — crosses, rosaries, Protestant/Catholic visual coding in enemy design.

### 6.2 Scottish Independence

**Situation.** Live political debate. 2014 referendum: 55% No / 45% Yes. 2016 Brexit (Scotland voted Remain, UK voted Leave). Multiple calls for second referendum; UK Supreme Court (2022) ruled Scottish Parliament cannot unilaterally call one.

**WHS practice:**
- **Do not** present the game as pro- or anti-independence.
- **Avoid** Union Jack vs Saltire visual conflict coding.
- **Avoid** political-party jokes (SNP / Labour / Conservative).
- **Safe ground:** Declaration of Arbroath (historical), Scott's Romantic nationalism (historical), Burns's egalitarianism ("A Man's a Man for a' That") — historical and broadly-shared.

### 6.3 Presbyterian / Catholic history

**Situation.** Scottish Reformation (1560) was violent and thorough. Covenanter persecution (see Part 2.4). Catholic communities preserved in Highlands (especially South Uist, Barra). Both traditions have living communities.

**WHS practice:**
- No mocking religion generally.
- Holy wells, St Columba, Iona monastery are universal-safe references.
- John Knox references should be careful (divisive figure).
- Avoid anti-clerical humour specifically targeting priests or ministers.

### 6.4 Jacobite / Hanoverian framing

See Part 2.3. Modern Scotland is generally neutral-to-positive on Jacobite memory (it's romantic), but specific depictions of Hanoverian brutality (Cumberland) need care.

### 6.5 Scotland in Europe

Scotland's relationship with the EU post-Brexit is a live discussion. References to Europe generally are safe; specific political statements should be avoided.

## Part 7 — Representing Living Communities

### 7.1 Glaswegian working-class

**Already in game:** Buckfast ned enemy, Glaswegian variant, urban ghaists.

**Getting it right:**
- **Still Game warmth** — Jack & Victor-style affection.
- **Limmy edge** — deadpan, self-aware.
- **Not Rab C Nesbitt caricature** — that character works because Gregor Fisher is *writing from inside*. Outsider Nesbitt-style mocking is flat.
- **Keep working-class dignity** — the buckfast-ned is a comic trope *inside* Glasgow, but he's also a person.

### 7.2 Highland rural communities

Currently underrepresented. Real Highland communities:
- Small, fragile, wrestling with depopulation.
- Tourism-hosting but not tourism-defined.
- Often multilingual (Gaelic + English).
- Crofters, fishermen, estate workers, environmental-sector workers.

**WHS opportunity:** a Highland-village NPC community (crofter, shepherd, schoolteacher, post-office-keeper, minister) depicted with real texture could be gorgeous.

### 7.3 Aberdonian fishing culture

Doric region. Distinct identity. Fisher-folk have specific vocabulary, dress, traditions. Stornoway Black Pudding, Arbroath Smokie are protected-origin products.

**Opportunity:** Doric Quinie variant (proposed in DESIGN_IDEAS) or a fisherman NPC treated authentically.

### 7.4 Orkney / Shetland communities

Norse-heritage. Up Helly Aa. Kirkwall/Lerwick distinct from mainland Scotland. Shetlanders often say "going south" to mean going to mainland Scotland.

**Opportunity:** Peerie Shetlander or Orcadian variants. Respectful Norse heritage (not caricatured).

### 7.5 Scottish Asian communities

Glasgow has significant Pakistani-Scottish community (Sanjeev Kohli of Still Game, Humza Yousaf former FM). Muslim Scots, Hindu Scots, Sikh Scots are part of modern Scotland.

**WHS opportunity:**
- Chicken Tikka Masala as a Scottish-invented dish (shipped reference).
- Consider non-white Scottish NPCs or haggis variants.
- **Avoid** religious/ethnic stereotyping.
- Humza Yousaf's history as first Muslim FM is Scottish history.

### 7.6 Scottish Jewish community

Edinburgh has historic Jewish community; Glasgow too. Smaller than Pakistani-Scottish but culturally present.

**WHS:** not a high-priority representation target, but respectful inclusion if natural.

### 7.7 LGBTQ+ Scots

Per Part 3.6. Representation welcome; no forced stereotyping.

### 7.8 Scottish-English

Many Scots live in England; some English live in Scotland. "Sassenach" (Saxon, outsider) is mild mock-term. Not racial; casual.

**WHS:** use "sassenach" banter sparingly and playfully.

### 7.9 Newly arrived communities

Scotland has refugee communities (Syrian, Ukrainian, Afghan post-2021). Working-class Scotland has generally been supportive. Modern Scotland is multicultural.

**WHS:** background NPC diversity can reflect this naturally.

## Part 8 — AI & Production Ethics

### 8.1 AI-generated content disclosure

Per `MUSIC_ART_TECH_RESEARCH.md` Part 10. Core principles for WHS:

- **Accept:** AI as research accelerator (this very doc), brainstorming partner, stem-drafting tool, code-assistance.
- **Reject:** AI as final author of shipped cultural content.
- **Disclose:** if AI-generated assets ship (for any reason), acknowledge in credits.

### 8.2 Cultural content and AI

Particular care for cultural content:

- **Never** ship AI-generated Gaelic without native speaker review.
- **Never** ship AI-generated Scots without native-speaker review.
- **Never** ship AI-generated Scottish imagery that could perpetuate stereotypes (e.g., AI-generated "Highlanders" tend to be fantasy-coded and ahistorical).
- **Always** credit human collaborators (translators, consultants, voice actors).

### 8.3 Cultural appropriation check

Before shipping anything involving a specific culture's material:
1. Have people from that culture reviewed it?
2. Are they credited (and paid, if appropriate)?
3. Would this feel respectful to someone from inside the culture?
4. If challenged, can we explain the choice with reference to community consultation?

### 8.4 The "Haggis Wildlife Foundation" problem

The wild haggis myth is *a shared Scottish joke*. Using it is fine (it's WHS's whole premise). But imagine if a Chinese developer made a game called "Wild Haggis Survivors" with no Scottish input — the warmth would shift. That's WHS's *actual* situation if no Scottish collaborators are involved.

**Recommendation:** have at least one Scottish collaborator on the project, credited visibly. Even as advisor. The warmth is partly *whose love* went into it.

### 8.5 Licensing and attribution

All third-party cultural material (ballads, recordings, translations, traditional music, consulted opinions) should have attribution in credits. "Special thanks" is cheap and generous.

## Part 9 — The Soul-Charter Filter

Every proposed Scottish content element can be filtered through three Soul Charter questions before shipping.

### 9.1 Warmth check

*Does this make the player feel the warmth of Scotland, or a cold use of Scotland-as-costume?*

- Warm: a Gran voice that comforts; a Burns quotation that lands; an accurate ceilidh callout.
- Cold: generic "Celtic" text; tartans without clan meaning; bagpipes without musical understanding.

### 9.2 Accuracy check

*Is this historically, linguistically, culturally accurate?*

- Yes: Bannockburn 1314; "Fit like?" means "how are you?" in Aberdeenshire; haggis contains oatmeal.
- No: kilts at Bannockburn; "och aye the noo" from a Shetland fisherman; Gaelic runes glow blue.

### 9.3 Dignity check

*Would this feel dignified to the people / culture represented?*

- Dignified: a Clearance-ruin biome with memorial plaque and banter that honours the lost.
- Undignified: "Haggis Hunter" enemies openly caricatured (WHS's game is *from the haggis's perspective*; the hunters are comic antagonists — this is fine).
- Undignified: a Traveller NPC stealing from the player; disability as "curse."

**If any check fails**, redesign.

## Part 10 — Practical Checklists

### 10.1 Before writing any Scottish-flavoured banter line

- [ ] Is the dialect / register clear? (Hearth / Edge / variant-specific)
- [ ] Does it sit inside Scottish self-mocking tradition (Still Game / Limmy)?
- [ ] Does it avoid tourist-Scotland tropes? (see VOICE_CARD anti-patterns)
- [ ] If Scots: is the spelling consistent with existing bilingual scheme?
- [ ] If Gaelic: has it been reviewed by a native speaker?
- [ ] Would a Scot reading it grin or wince?

### 10.2 Before designing a historical-themed biome or event

- [ ] Is the historical event <100 years ago? (If yes, likely avoid)
- [ ] Is this a trauma for a specific community still living with it? (If yes, Grave tone only; consult)
- [ ] Is the named site real? (Research to verify geography, atmosphere)
- [ ] Are the community's memorial practices respected? (Cite them in banter/plaques)
- [ ] Can we point to a responsible precedent? (Nan Shepherd's nature writing; Neil Gunn's fiction; memorial-statue artists)

### 10.3 Before using a real-world brand reference

- [ ] Is this protected trademark?
- [ ] Have we considered genericisation or licensing?
- [ ] Does our use imply endorsement, defame, or disparage?
- [ ] Has legal review been done (if commercial)?

### 10.4 Before depicting a specific community

- [ ] Have we consulted a member of that community?
- [ ] Is the depiction drawn from their own voice (interviews, writings, music)?
- [ ] Is it stereotype-aware?
- [ ] Is it dignified?
- [ ] Is attribution given?

### 10.5 Before translating into Gaelic or Scots

- [ ] Is the translator a native speaker?
- [ ] Is the translation paid?
- [ ] Does it preserve tone and meaning, not just words?
- [ ] Is it credited?

### 10.6 Before writing about living persons

- [ ] Is this inspiration (broad voice register) or impersonation (specific mimicry)?
- [ ] Does it avoid defamation?
- [ ] If recently deceased: is it respectful (estate-appropriate)?
